## Description

Playful AI with IoT and Generative AI introduces high school teachers to artificial intelligence through hands-on exploration of microcontrollers and large language models. Using CS Unplugged activities to ground abstract concepts in physical experience, teachers then connect those ideas to real IoT devices and experiment with generative AI tools, prompting, querying, and integrating LLMs into simple sensor-driven systems. No prior AI background is required: participants leave with ready-to-run classroom activities, a concrete understanding of how AI perceives and responds to the physical world, and the enthusiasm to bring playful, critical AI literacy into their own teaching.



## Original - Didactic Workshop IOT

**Playful AI with IoT and Generative AI** is a two-day hands-on workshop that invites high school teachers to explore artificial intelligence not as a distant technical subject, but as something tangible, surprising, and worth arguing about. The workshop is designed around the principle of didactic transposition: the process by which scholarly knowledge is deconstructed and rebuilt into content that can actually be taught. AI and IoT are fields where the gap between expert knowledge and classroom practice is wide, and bridging that gap thoughtfully is the central challenge the workshop takes on.

Participants do not simply learn about AI. They experience the process of transposition from the inside, first encountering concepts as a learner would, through physical activity, productive confusion, and hands-on experimentation, and then stepping back to ask how those same concepts could be made accessible, honest, and genuinely engaging for their own students.

**Day 1** starts with a deceptively simple question: Is this AI? and follows the thread from everyday objects through microcontrollers, sensor data, and the philosophy of machine perception. CS Unplugged activities serve here as a deliberate tool for transposition: they strip away the technical scaffolding of real AI systems to expose the underlying ideas in a form anyone can reason about. Participants build their first sensing device, run a hands-on classification activity using nothing but labels and voting, and close the day by sketching something they would actually want to bring into their own classroom.

**Day 2** shifts from the physical to the linguistic. A group storytelling game unpacks how large language models actually work (tokens, probabilities, and all) before participants dive into the art of prompting and discover how much craft and creativity the right instruction requires. The centrepiece activity connects a live sensor to an LLM and asks it to narrate, explain, or invent a story about what it detects. A critical literacy session confronts the machine's confident failures head-on. Throughout, participants are asked not only what they are learning, but what has been simplified, what has been lost in translation, and what a responsible transposition of AI into the high school classroom looks like.

The workshop closes with a design sprint in which participants invent, playtest, and steal classroom activities from each other, applying didactic transposition directly: taking something they have just learned as a novice and rebuilding it into something a student could genuinely encounter and explore.

No prior AI background is required. Participants leave with ready-to-run classroom activities, a concrete, embodied understanding of how AI perceives and responds to the world, and the tools to make it truly teachable.

Here's the revised topic list, aligned with the expanded description, including the didactic transposition layer:

#### Day 1 — Waking Up the Machine: From Ideas to Connected Things

1. **"Is this AI?"** — A provocative CS Unplugged opener where teachers sort everyday objects and behaviours into AI / not AI, argue their case, and discover how slippery the concept really is. The first act of didactic transposition: what do we actually mean by this idea, and how would we explain it to someone who has never heard the term?
2. **The world as data** — How microcontrollers experience reality through sensors: a hands-on "blind tasting" where devices identify mystery environments (dark cupboard? sunny windowsill? someone breathing nearby?). Participants experience sensing as a learner would, before asking how to make that experience teachable.
3. **Build your first sensing thing** — Plug in, wire up, make it blink: a low-stakes, high-delight first circuit with a sensor of your choice. Productive confusion is welcome here; getting things wrong is part of the method.
4. **Teach a machine with sticky notes** — Unplugged activity: participants physically label, sort, and vote on data points to train a paper classifier, then watch it fail in interesting ways. A deliberate transposition tool: stripping away technical scaffolding to expose the underlying idea in a form anyone can reason about.
5. **When does a thermostat become intelligent?** — A guided philosophical provocation using everyday objects to interrogate what "sensing", "deciding", and "learning" actually mean. Participants begin asking not only what AI is, but what has been simplified and what has been lost in translation.
6. **Reflection jam** — Participants sketch, doodle, or build a quick prototype of something they would want a sensing device to notice in their classroom. The first attempt at transposing what they have learned into something a student could genuinely encounter and explore.

#### Day 2 — Talking to the Machine: Generative AI in the Wild

1. **LLMs in plain language** — Tokens, probabilities, and hallucinations explained through a group storytelling game where participants predict the next word. Encountering the concept as a learner first, before asking how to teach it.
2. **The art of the prompt** — A creative prompting challenge: who can get the most surprising, useful, or absurd response from the same starting sentence? Participants discover how much craft and creativity the right instruction requires.
3. **Give your sensor a voice** — Hands-on: pipe live sensor readings into an LLM and ask it to narrate, explain, or invent a story about what it is detecting. The centrepiece activity where the physical and linguistic come together.
4. **The lying, confident machine** — A critical AI literacy activity exploring bias, hallucination, and opacity through deliberately broken or misleading LLM outputs. Participants confront what a responsible transposition of AI into the high school classroom must honestly include.
5. **Classroom activity design sprint** — In small groups, participants apply didactic transposition directly: taking something they have just learned as a novice and rebuilding it into a playful IoT + GenAI activity for their own students. Constraints: it must be cheap, safe to fail, and genuinely fun.
6. **Playtest and steal** — Groups swap activities, try each other's ideas, and give feedback as if they were the students. Testing whether the transposition actually works in practice.
7. **What do we take home?** — Closing reflection: one thing that surprised you, one thing you will try, one question you are still sitting with. And underneath all three: what does this idea need to become, for it to be truly teachable?

Because the micro:bit’s processor only has megabytes of storage, large models cannot be loaded onto it. Instead, bridge the two environments:

- **Serial Communication (Python):** Use a pyserial script on a computer to read live data from the micro:bit's sensors (accelerometer, temperature, light). This data can be fed into an AI model loaded from the [Hugging Face Hub](https://huggingface.co/) to generate text, synthesize speech, or perform complex classifications.
- **Edge Impulse Integration:** You can train a machine learning model on Edge Impulse using data from the micro:bit, download the resulting model, and flash it onto the micro:bit using MicroPython or MakeCode.
